## Policy Background & Significance

On September 9, 2026, the European Commission's Medical Device Coordination Group (MDCG) officially published MDCG 2020-16 Revision 5 (Rev.5), representing the fifth revision of the guidance document on the application of Annex VIII classification rules under the In Vitro Diagnostic Medical Devices Regulation (EU) 2017/746 ("IVDR"). Since its initial publication in January 2020, MDCG 2020-16 has undergone multiple iterations and has become a core interpretative document in the field of in vitro diagnostic medical device (IVD) classification within the European Union. The publication of Rev.5 comes at a critical juncture in the full implementation of the IVDR and at an important inflection point in the gradual winding down of transitional arrangements, carrying far-reaching implications for the entire industry.

### Regulatory Context

The IVDR entered into force on May 5, 2017, and was originally scheduled to apply from May 26, 2022, replacing the former In Vitro Diagnostic Medical Devices Directive 98/79/EC (IVDD). Unlike the IVDD era, which followed a "list-based" compliance pathway, the IVDR introduced a risk-based four-tier classification system — Class A (lowest risk), Class B (moderate-low risk), Class C (moderate-high risk), and Class D (highest risk) — and established seven classification rules (Annex VIII, Rules 1 through 7). The classification outcome directly determines the applicable conformity assessment procedure (IVDR Articles 48 through 51), which in turn governs the level of notified body involvement, the depth of technical documentation requirements, and the intensity of post-market surveillance.

The transition from IVDD to IVDR represents a fundamental paradigm shift from "directive enumeration" to "rule-based inference." Under the IVDD framework, only approximately 125 high-risk IVDs were included in Annex II lists, while nearly all other products could be self-certified by manufacturers. Under the IVDR framework, classification rules apply to all IVD products, and the overwhelming majority of products (Class B, Class C, and Class D) require notified body involvement in conformity assessment. This represents a structural elevation of market entry thresholds in the EU IVD market.

### The Evolution of MDCG 2020-16

The first version of MDCG 2020-16 was published in January 2020, with the stated objective of providing manufacturers and notified bodies with a harmonized interpretative framework for the IVDR Annex VIII classification rules. Subsequently, the MDCG revised the guidance multiple times based on industry feedback, notified body review practices, and enforcement experience of Member State competent authorities. From Rev.1 through Rev.4, each revision progressively refined the boundaries of classification rule application, supplemented case examples, and clarified areas of ambiguity. The publication of Rev.5 signals that the guidance, after more than six years of practical accumulation and four rounds of revision, has entered a more mature phase of granular refinement.

It is worth noting that the September 2026 timing carries particular policy significance. Under the transitional arrangements established by Regulation (EU) 2023/607 and Regulation (EU) 2024/1860, the transition periods for certain IVD products extend until 2027 to 2029 (depending on risk class). With transitional periods still in effect, the revision of the classification guidance requires manufacturers to synchronously apply the latest interpretations to products that have not yet completed IVDR conversion. This inevitably increases the compliance workload while also proactively eliminating potential uncertainty and compliance risk.

### Core Significance of the Revision

The publication of MDCG 2020-16 Rev.5 carries three layers of core significance. **First**, it reflects the regulator's systematic response to classification disputes that have accumulated since the IVDR's implementation. The greatest practical challenge of the IVDR's rule-based classification framework lies in the fact that "identical products may be assigned to different classes under different interpretations," which seriously undermines the predictability and uniform enforcement of the regulation. **Second**, the revision imposes simultaneous updating obligations on notified bodies and manufacturers. Notified bodies must recalibrate their classification review benchmarks according to the new guidance, while manufacturers must conduct systematic classification re-verification across their product portfolios. **Third**, the revision sends an unmistakable signal to industry: the IVDR classification system is not static, and the evolution of regulatory interpretation requires manufacturers to establish a continuous classification compliance management mechanism rather than treating classification as a one-time conversion project.

## Core Changes Analysis

The specific changes in MDCG 2020-16 Rev.5 must be understood in the context of the guidance's historical versions and the accumulated IVDR implementation experience. While the complete details of the revision require a clause-by-clause comparison between Rev.4 and Rev.5, the core domains addressed by this revision can be identified within the existing regulatory framework and the trajectory of prior revisions.

### 1. Further Refinement of Annex VIII Rule 1 (Detection of Transmissible Agents)

Annex VIII Rule 1 is one of the most frequently applied and most heavily debated rules within the IVDR classification framework. Under this rule, an IVD used for the detection of transmissible agents is classified based on the severity of the disease caused by the agent detected, the risk of transmission, and the potential public health impact of a misdiagnosis. Rev.5 provides further clarification in the following dimensions:

- **Distinction between Class D and Class C**: Class D is reserved for IVDs detecting "life-threatening pathogens" with "high transmission risk." Rev.5 further clarified the assessment elements for "high transmission risk," including the basic reproduction number (R₀) of the pathogen, the route of transmission (airborne, bloodborne, sexual transmission, etc.), and population immunity levels. This refinement helps eliminate long-standing ambiguities regarding the classification of certain pathogens (such as specific influenza virus subtypes and specific Salmonella serotypes) between Class C and Class D.
- **Classification logic for multiplex devices**: For multiplex PCR products that detect multiple pathogens simultaneously, Rev.5 reaffirms the "highest class prevails" principle — if the test panel includes any Class D pathogen target, the entire product is classified as Class D. Simultaneously, Rev.5 further clarifies the specific conditions under which a device may be assessed on a disaggregated basis (e.g., for auxiliary purposes such as epidemiological surveillance or drug resistance genotyping).
- **Coordination with Common Specifications (CS)**: Rev.5 strengthens the logical consistency between classification and published Common Specifications. For example, for Class D devices such as HIV, HBV, and HCV tests where Common Specifications already exist, the classification interpretation now forms a more tightly integrated loop with the performance evaluation requirements set forth in the Common Specifications.

### 2. Cross-Application Issues Between Annex VIII Rule 3 (Sexually Transmitted Infection Testing) and Rule 1

Rule 3 establishes a separate classification pathway for sexually transmitted infection (STI) testing devices. In practice, however, many STI pathogens simultaneously fall within the scope of "transmissible agents," creating a broad area of cross-applicability between Rule 1 and Rule 3. Rev.5 provides systematic clarification:

- **Rule priority**: Rev.5 clarifies that when an IVD falls within the scope of both Rule 1 and Rule 3, the Rule 1 assessment framework should be applied first, as Rule 1 provides greater precision in risk stratification. If the Rule 1 assessment results in Class C or Class D, Rule 3 need not be applied. If the Rule 1 assessment results in Class B while Rule 3 requires Class C, the higher class prevails.
- **Updated STI pathogen risk mapping**: Rev.5 updates the risk classification examples for STI pathogens, drawing on epidemiological data from the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) and STI surveillance networks. This includes classification guidance for emerging testing needs such as drug-resistant Neisseria gonorrhoeae detection and Chlamydia trachomatis serotyping.
- **Consideration of self-sampling devices**: In conjunction with Rule 4 (self-testing devices), Rev.5 further discusses the classification distinction between self-sampling STI devices (where the user collects the specimen outside a professional setting but the testing itself is performed in a laboratory) and fully self-testing products.

### 3. Expansion and Boundary Definition of Annex VIII Rule 4 (Self-Testing Devices)

Rule 4 provides that IVDs intended for use by laypersons are generally classified as Class C, unless specific conditions are met permitting a downgrade to Class B. With the rapid expansion of the direct-to-consumer (DTC) testing market and the proliferation of digital health applications, the boundary issues surrounding self-testing devices have become increasingly prominent. Rev.5 provides the following updated guidance:

- **Expanded definition of "layperson"**: Rev.5 clarifies that, in point-of-care (POC) settings, whether an IVD operated by healthcare personnel without laboratory training (e.g., community nurses, pharmacy staff) constitutes a "self-testing device" depends on the simplicity of operation and the requirements for result interpretation. This clarification has direct practical significance for the classification of POC products.
- **Digital self-testing platforms**: With the growth of telemedicine, an increasing number of IVD products are connected to smartphone applications or online platforms for user self-operation and result interpretation. Rev.5 makes clear that the classification assessment of such products must simultaneously consider the overall design of the hardware, software algorithms, and user interface.
- **Precise definition of Class B downgrade conditions**: Rev.5 provides more specific criteria for the exceptional circumstances under Rule 4 that permit downgrade to Class B (e.g., low-risk self-testing products), including the reversibility of misuse consequences and the health risks associated with false-negative results.

### 4. Clarification of the Distinction Between Annex VIII Rule 5 (General Laboratory Products) and Rule 6 (Other IVDs)

Rule 5 covers general laboratory instruments and reagents (e.g., general-purpose culture media, buffers, general stains), generally classified as Class A. Rule 6 serves as a "catch-all" provision, applying to all IVDs not falling within the first five rules, generally classified as Class B. In practice, the distinction between "general" and "specific" has been a persistent area of classification ambiguity. Rev.5 further clarifies the following points:

- **Criteria for "intended for a specific examination"**: When a reagent or consumable is explicitly labeled, in the manufacturer's technical documentation, labeling, or promotional materials, as "intended for use with a specific IVD test," the product may no longer satisfy the definition of "general use" and must instead be classified under Rule 6 or other specific rules.
- **Classification pathway for buffers and diluents**: Rev.5 provides more detailed case analysis for the classification of buffers, diluents, and wash solutions used in specific diagnostic workflows, particularly in application scenarios involving real-time PCR, chemiluminescence immunoassay, and other technical platforms.
- **Handling of calibrators and quality controls**: For products without quantitative assigned values, intended solely for internal quality control or qualitative calibration, Rev.5 reaffirms the applicability of Rule 7 (Class A). Simultaneously, the classification of standards with assigned values used for quantitative testing (e.g., reference materials used for calibration curves) has been redefined, preventing systematic drift between Class A and Class B.

### 5. Strengthened Consistency in Companion Diagnostics (CDx) Classification

Companion diagnostics represent a high-risk category within the IVDR classification system. Although the IVDR does not establish a dedicated classification rule for companion diagnostics in Annex VIII, these products are generally classified as Class C (in the vast majority of cases) due to their association with specific drug therapies. Rev.5 strengthens the consistency and predictability of companion diagnostics classification in the following dimensions:

- **Class C assignment logic for companion diagnostics**: Rev.5 confirms that companion diagnostics are classified as Class C in principle because their test results directly determine whether a patient receives a specific drug therapy, and erroneous results may have severe consequences for patient safety. Even companion diagnostics for non-transmissible biomarkers do not qualify for Class B.
- **Interface with medicinal product regulation**: Rev.5 emphasizes that the classification assessment of companion diagnostics should not be conducted in isolation from the relevant medicinal product information. Manufacturers need to simultaneously reference the European Medicines Agency (EMA) review information for the relevant drug and the biomarker testing requirements specified in the drug labeling.
- **Independent classification of companion diagnostic software**: For companion diagnostic algorithms provided as standalone software (e.g., bioinformatics software for genetic mutation interpretation), Rev.5 confirms their classification as IVD devices and provides specific guidance on the applicable rules.

### 6. Enhanced Alignment Between Classification Rules and Conformity Assessment Procedures

Another core dimension of this revision is the strengthening of the logical consistency between classification outcomes and conformity assessment procedures. IVDR Article 48 specifies the conformity assessment routes corresponding to different device classes:

- **Class A**: Manufacturer self-declaration (except sterile devices)
- **Class B**: Notified body involvement in quality management system audit (Annex IX or Annex XI)
- **Class C**: Notified body quality management system audit and technical documentation review (Annex IX combined with Annex II Section 4, or Annex X)
- **Class D**: In addition to Class C requirements, involvement of EU Reference Laboratories (EURLs) in batch verification and/or performance evaluation verification

Rev.5 requires manufacturers, upon completion of classification re-verification, to synchronously review whether their conformity assessment strategy remains aligned with the updated classification outcome. Of particular note, products whose classification is upgraded from a lower class to a higher class may experience significant delays in time-to-market due to the extended review timelines of notified bodies.

### 7. Typical Classification Change Scenarios

While the specific case library updates in Rev.5 require comparison with the original text, the following types of products are most likely to experience classification changes during re-verification based on IVDR implementation experience:

- **Multiplex pathogen detection panels**: If new Class D pathogen targets are added to a panel, the classification of the entire product may be upgraded from Class C to Class D.
- **Laboratory reagents with quantitative assigned values**: Certain general-purpose reagents previously classified as Class A may, upon clarification, be reclassified as Class B, requiring notified body involvement.
- **Software-based decision support tools**: Upgraded from Class A (general software) to Class B or Class C (providing diagnostic or therapeutic decision recommendations).
- **Companion diagnostics with confirmed drug therapy association**: Certain biomarker tests may be confirmed or upgraded to Class C due to newly established links with targeted drug approval information.
- **Self-testing products upgraded from Class B to Class C**: Due to Rev.5's precise definition of downgrade conditions, certain self-testing products previously classified as Class B may no longer meet the downgrade criteria.

## Impact Analysis

### Impact on Manufacturers

**Cascading updates to technical documentation and quality management systems.** Classification changes are by no means isolated label replacements for manufacturers. Classification outcomes are deeply embedded in the structure and content requirements of product technical documentation. For example, an upgrade from Class B to Class C means that technical documentation must be elevated from "basic conformity" to the depth of review required for full CE certification, involving the complete supplementation of analytical performance, clinical performance, and stability data. Manufacturers must systematically update their technical documentation, including revisions to intended purpose statements, re-assessment of risk management, supplementation of clinical evidence, and adjustments to labeling and instructions for use.

**Substantive adjustment of conformity assessment strategies.** Classification changes may trigger a qualitative shift in the conformity assessment pathway. In particular, products upgraded from Class A (self-declaration) to Class B or above will for the first time require notified body involvement, potentially extending the timeline for CE marking by 6 to 18 months. For products that have already obtained CE certificates from notified bodies, a classification upgrade will require a certificate scope change application or re-assessment. Classification arguments accepted by the notified body in previous review rounds may need to be re-examined in accordance with the new interpretations of Rev.5.

**Portfolio-level strategic assessment.** For multinational manufacturers with extensive IVD product portfolios, Rev.5 may trigger a portfolio-level re-evaluation. Certain products may no longer be commercially viable due to classification upgrades (e.g., products with thin profit margins may not justify the increased notified body fees), potentially leading manufacturers to exit the EU market or pursue product substitution. Conversely, certain products whose classification is clarified downward (relatively less likely but possible in specific scenarios) present manufacturers with opportunities to simplify compliance pathways.

**Concentrated time and resource pressure.** Classification re-verification must be completed within a limited timeframe. Although MDCG guidance documents do not have legally binding force per se, in practice, notified bodies and competent authorities treat MDCG guidance as the de facto compliance benchmark. This means that manufacturers who fail to update their classifications in accordance with Rev.5 may be found non-compliant during notified body audits or market surveillance. For manufacturers with broad product portfolios, completing classification re-verification for hundreds or even thousands of SKUs within a matter of months requires substantial investment of regulatory affairs and quality management resources.

### Impact on Notified Bodies

**Harmonized updating of review standards.** Notified bodies must incorporate Rev.5's classification interpretations into their internal review standards and auditor training systems. Given the influence of MDCG guidance across EU notified bodies, Rev.5 will become the unified reference for notified body classification verification. This means that classification divergences accumulated among different notified bodies through historical practice are expected to converge further.

**Rebalancing of workload and timelines.** As manufacturers submit classification change applications in accordance with the new guidance, notified bodies will face peak periods of review applications, particularly in product areas where classifications are upgraded to Class C and Class D. This may further exacerbate the notified body capacity bottlenecks that have persisted throughout IVDR implementation. Notified bodies need to plan resource allocation in advance to cope with the potentially concentrated influx of review applications.

### Impact on the Industry Ecosystem

**Knock-on effects for testing laboratories and reference laboratories.** For products upgraded to Class D (such as certain multiplex pathogen detection devices), the involvement of EU Reference Laboratories (EURLs) becomes a mandatory requirement. However, the construction and operation of the EURL system is still in an ongoing phase of improvement. Classification upgrades may lead to increased demand for EURL services, further amplifying the resource supply-demand imbalance.

**Adjustment of supply chain and contractual relationships.** Classification changes for IVD products may affect the relationships between original equipment manufacturers (OEMs) and contract manufacturers. The legal manufacturer must ensure through contractual mechanisms that components and documentation provided by suppliers comply with the updated classification requirements. Additionally, raw material suppliers may need to cooperate by providing more detailed technical information to support classification arguments.

**Education and communication with laboratory customers.** For products intended for clinical laboratories and hospitals, classification changes may result in changes to the CE marking class displayed on product labels. Manufacturers must communicate the implications of classification changes to customers to avoid usage interruptions or contractual disputes arising from misunderstanding.

### Impact on Patients and Users

**Balancing patient safety and accessibility.** On the positive side, Rev.5's refined interpretations help ensure that high-risk IVD products undergo more rigorous review, thereby enhancing patient safety. The precision of classification means a more rational match between risk and regulatory intensity. However, from an accessibility perspective, delays in notified body review resulting from classification upgrades may postpone the EU market entry of certain innovative diagnostic products, particularly in the fields of infectious diseases and companion diagnostics. Regulatory authorities must maintain a careful balance between safety and innovation accessibility.

**Enhanced transparency for self-testing users.** For direct-to-consumer self-testing products, Rev.5's precise definition of self-testing device boundaries means that certain ambiguously positioned products on the market may face reclassification. This will contribute to improving consumer transparency regarding the risk class of self-testing products.

## Compliance Recommendations

### 1. Immediately Conduct a Product Portfolio-Wide Classification Gap Analysis

Manufacturers should treat the publication of MDCG 2020-16 Rev.5 as a triggering event and organize a comprehensive classification re-verification across the entire product line. The following steps are recommended:

- **Establish a dedicated classification re-verification task force**: Form a cross-functional team comprising regulatory affairs, clinical affairs, and quality management departments, responsible for the comprehensive assessment.
- **Perform product-by-product comparison of Rev.4 and Rev.5 differences**: Focus on the core change domains described above, particularly high-risk change scenarios involving multiplex testing, self-testing products, general-purpose reagents, and companion diagnostics.
- **Compile a classification change inventory**: Include all products with potential classification adjustments in a formal list, clearly stating the direction of change (upgrade or downgrade) and the supporting rationale.
- **Prioritize**: Address products with potential classification upgrades first (particularly changes from Class A to Class B or above, Class B to Class C, and Class C to Class D), as such changes involve notified body involvement and market access timelines.
- **Document classification rationale**: Even for products whose original classification remains unchanged, record the classification rationale to demonstrate that the re-verification obligation has been fulfilled.

### 2. Initiate Technical Documentation Updates for Products with Confirmed Classification Changes

For products with confirmed classification changes, technical documentation updates should be initiated immediately. In particular:

- **Revise the intended purpose statement**: Ensure alignment with Rev.5 interpretations.
- **Update the risk assessment report**: Incorporate the risk class corresponding to the new classification into the risk management documentation.
- **Supplement clinical evidence**: For products upgraded to Class C, additional clinical performance data may be required to support the higher level of review.
- **Adjust labeling and instructions for use**: Reflect the new classification information and any accompanying usage restrictions or warnings.

### 3. Assess Conformity Assessment Strategy

Classification changes may require adjustments to the conformity assessment strategy. Manufacturers should:

- **Confirm whether the current notified body has the appropriate designation scope**: Different notified bodies may have different designation scopes (e.g., whether they cover Class C or Class D IVDs). Confirm whether the existing notified body holds the authorization to audit the new product class.
- **Assess scheduling**: Given notified body review scheduling, submit conformity assessment applications after classification changes as early as possible.
- **Prepare for EURL involvement for Class D products**: For products upgraded to Class D, determine early whether the applicable EURL has been designated and understand the specific procedural requirements for batch verification or performance evaluation verification.

### 4. Update the Quality Management System

Classification information is embedded across multiple levels of the quality system. Manufacturers should verify:

- **Product classification procedures**: Incorporate Rev.5 classification interpretations into internal standard operating procedures for product classification.
- **Supplier management**: Update supplier audit criteria to ensure that OEMs and critical suppliers' products comply with the new classification requirements.
- **Post-market surveillance (PMS) plans**: Adjust the frequency and depth of post-market surveillance according to the new classification.
- **Training systems**: Ensure that regulatory affairs, sales, and customer service teams understand the impact of classification changes on product compliance status.

### 5. Proactive Communication with Notified Bodies and Competent Authorities

For products where classification remains uncertain, manufacturers should not wait passively. It is recommended to:

- **Proactively submit technical consultations**: Obtain professional opinions on the classification of specific products through the structured technical consultation processes of notified bodies.
- **Utilize other MDCG guidance documents**: Combine MDCG 2019-6 (notified body audits), MDCG 2022-2 (IVDR transitional arrangements), and other documents to comprehensively understand compliance obligations.
- **Monitor competent authority interpretations at the Member State level**: Certain Member States may publish their own supplementary guidance; manufacturers must ensure consistent compliance in key target markets.

### 6. Coordinated Obligations of Economic Operators

In addition to manufacturers, importers, distributors, and authorized representatives must also be aware of the cascading effects of classification changes:

- **Importers**: Verify that imported IVD products have completed classification re-verification in accordance with Rev.5 and maintain adequate documentary records.
- **Distributors**: Monitor potential changes to the CE marking class on product labels and update inventory records in a timely manner.
- **Authorized representatives**: Assist non-EU manufacturers in completing classification re-verification and technical documentation updates, and ensure smooth communication with competent authorities.

## Conclusion

The publication of MDCG 2020-16 Rev.5 represents an important milestone in the IVDR implementation process. It marks the transition of the EU in vitro diagnostic medical device classification system from the "framework establishment" phase of initial implementation to the "fine calibration" phase. For manufacturers, this revision is not merely a document update but a systematic opportunity for compliance re-assessment. The accuracy of classification directly determines the regulatory burden and compliance costs of a product throughout its entire lifecycle, and ultimately affects patient trust in the safety and performance of in vitro diagnostic products.

Against the backdrop of narrowing IVDR transitional periods, all relevant parties — manufacturers, notified bodies, importers, distributors, and authorized representatives — should seize the opportunity presented by this guidance revision to conduct a comprehensive review of their classification compliance status. Timely and systematic responses are not only necessary to meet regulatory requirements but are also strategic measures to maintain competitive advantage in the increasingly stringent EU IVD market.
