## 1. Policy Background and Significance

On 11 November 2026, the European Commission's Directorate-General for Health and Food Safety (DG SANTE), the European Medicines Agency (EMA), and Singapore's Health Sciences Authority (HSA) formally signed a Working Arrangement on public health collaboration. Centered on medicines and medical devices, the document establishes an institutionalized mechanism for cross-jurisdictional information exchange and regulatory cooperation. Within the broader evolution of global medical device regulatory governance, this Working Arrangement is by no means an isolated event—it represents a significant step in the European Union's strategy to deepen international regulatory cooperation following the full implementation of the Medical Devices Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2017/745, hereafter "EU MDR").

From a policy background perspective, since the promulgation of EU MDR and the In Vitro Diagnostic Medical Devices Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2017/746, IVDR) in 2017, the EU has been systematically rebuilding its medical device regulatory framework. The strict post-market surveillance (PMS) requirements, clinical evaluation standards, Unique Device Identification (UDI) system, and the European Database on Medical Devices (EUDAMED) established under EU MDR have significantly raised compliance thresholds and increased the density of regulatory data. However, the regulatory resources of any single jurisdiction are inherently limited. In an era of globally dispersed supply chains, the capacity for early identification of product safety signals and cross-market responsiveness has become a critical bottleneck for regulatory effectiveness. It is precisely this reality that has driven the European Commission to accelerate the establishment of institutionalized cooperation channels with like-minded third-country regulators.

Singapore's strategic positioning in this landscape is particularly prominent. As one of the most capable and internationally recognized health regulatory agencies in the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) region, the Singapore Health Sciences Authority (HSA) has long been regarded as a "reference agency" by multiple ASEAN member states. HSA exercises full-lifecycle regulatory oversight over medical devices under the Health Products Act (Cap 122D) and the Health Products (Medical Devices) Regulations 2010, with regulatory science capabilities and review standards that closely align with international best practices. Singapore is also a member of the International Medical Device Regulators Forum (IMDRF) and plays a leading role in advancing the implementation of the ASEAN Medical Device Directive (AMDD). The EU's choice of Singapore as its first such cooperation partner in the ASEAN region thus carries clear strategic intent—using Singapore as a fulcrum to extend regulatory influence across the entire ASEAN market.

From a legal nature perspective, this Working Arrangement is an administrative cooperation instrument of a "soft law" character, rather than a legally binding treaty or Mutual Recognition Agreement (MRA). This means it does not directly alter manufacturers' statutory obligations, nor does it automatically generate exemptions or simplifications in market access. However, underestimating its practical impact would be a serious misjudgment. Historical experience demonstrates that EU working arrangements with third-country regulators often constitute the "preliminary infrastructure" for subsequent technical harmonization, data recognition, and even joint review procedures. For medical device manufacturers serving both the EU and ASEAN markets, the institutionalized information-exchange channels established by this Arrangement may, in the medium term, substantively affect regulatory inspections, safety signal assessments, and clinical data recognition practices.

From a broader perspective, this Working Arrangement also represents a concrete projection of the EU's "Global Gateway" strategy into the health regulatory domain. By establishing deep cooperation with capable and value-aligned third-country regulators, the EU is progressively building a global medical device regulatory collaboration network anchored in EU MDR as the core benchmark. For industry stakeholders, early recognition of this trend and proactive adjustment of compliance strategies is essential to reducing multi-market compliance costs and avoiding reactive adaptation.

## 2. Core Changes Analysis

Although the Working Arrangement is a framework document, the three core cooperation mechanisms it establishes carry substantial practical content that merits detailed examination.

### (1) Establishment of an Institutionalized Information Exchange Mechanism

The Working Arrangement expressly identifies information exchange as the primary area of cooperation, encompassing product safety, efficacy, quality, clinical-trial authorization, labelling, and policy development. This is the most operationally significant clause in the entire document.

In the medical device context, the exchange of "product safety information" means that when one regulatory authority identifies a safety signal for a specific device—such as trends in serious adverse events, material biocompatibility issues, or cybersecurity vulnerabilities—it may notify the other party under this Arrangement. For products registered in both the EU and Singapore, such cross-jurisdictional safety information flows can significantly shorten the global response time for safety signals. With reference to EU MDR Articles 83 through 86 on post-market surveillance systems, PMS plans, and Periodic Safety Update Reports (PSURs), as well as Articles 87 through 92 on serious incident reporting, trend reporting, and field safety corrective actions (FSCA), manufacturers already bear highly systematic data collection and reporting obligations in the EU. The signing of this Working Arrangement means that safety signals revealed by such data may, at the regulatory level, be cross-verified with Singapore's regulatory database with greater efficiency in the future.

The exchange of "clinical-trial authorization" information equally merits close attention. EU MDR Articles 62 through 82 establish stringent application and review procedures for clinical investigations, while Singapore's clinical trial regulatory framework also maintains high standards. The exchange of clinical trial authorization information between the parties may influence manufacturers' strategic decisions when conducting clinical research in both jurisdictions—for example, whether a clinical trial authorization decision obtained in the EU may serve as referential information in HSA's review. While no mechanism currently suggests direct recognition exists, the very flow of information is sufficient to influence the practical efficiency of regulatory review.

The exchange of "labelling" information touches upon one of the most nuanced aspects of manufacturer compliance. Annex I of EU MDR sets forth detailed requirements for labels and instructions for use, while Singapore adopts risk classification and labelling standards based on the IMDRF framework. Information sharing on labelling policies between the parties may, in the future, catalyze a convergence trend in labelling formats and content, potentially bringing convenience to manufacturers seeking label standardization.

### (2) Establishment of an Observer and Expert Participation Mechanism

The second core mechanism of the Working Arrangement permits each party to participate as observers or send experts to the other's scientific committees, multilateral procedures, and "cluster activities." The deeper significance of this mechanism lies in fostering mutual understanding of regulatory approaches and joint capacity-building in regulatory science.

Within the EU MDR framework, EMA's role in medical devices is expanding, particularly in the coordination of expert panels for high-risk devices (EU MDR Article 106) and certain functions in the operation of EUDAMED. HSA experts participating as observers in relevant scientific discussions will not only enhance understanding of EU review logic but may also, over time, lead Singapore's review standards to converge toward EU MDR benchmarks. Conversely, EU regulators participating in HSA-led ASEAN regional activities will help the EU understand the unique regulatory environment and clinical practice differences of the ASEAN market.

The concept of "cluster activities" merits particular attention. In EU regulatory cooperation parlance, "cluster activities" generally refer to thematic working groups or joint workshop activities organized around specific topics, such as artificial intelligence-enabled medical devices, combination products, or companion diagnostics. This indicates that cooperation between the parties will not be broad and generic but will focus on specific technical domains and regulatory topics through targeted, in-depth collaboration. For manufacturers, monitoring which "clusters" are prioritized for activation serves as an important indicator of the actual direction of regulatory cooperation.

### (3) Establishment of Training and Knowledge-Sharing Programmes

The third core mechanism is training and knowledge-sharing, aimed at enhancing regulatory science expertise on both sides and promoting best practices. In the medical device regulatory field, regulatory science spans multiple highly specialized branches, ranging from preclinical evaluation, biocompatibility assessment, and software validation to the application of real-world evidence (RWE). While cross-national sharing of such knowledge does not directly alter legal texts, it will gradually produce a "substantive effect" of regulatory convergence through the enhancement of reviewer expertise and the harmonization of review methodologies.

Specifically, training cooperation may cover EU MDR clinical evaluation requirements, EUDAMED operational logic, review frameworks for AI-enabled medical devices, and the application of real-world data in post-market surveillance. For EU manufacturers seeking to use Singapore as a springboard into the ASEAN market, or Singapore-based manufacturers intending to enter the EU market, this methodological convergence implies that the "translation cost" of cross-market compliance may progressively diminish.

### (4) Immediate Effect and Flexible Adjustment Mechanism

The Working Arrangement expressly states that it "applies immediately" and will be reviewed periodically, with the possibility of extension or adaptation by mutual agreement. Unlike many international cooperation agreements that require lengthy domestic approval procedures, such administrative working arrangements offer the advantage of rapid activation and substantial flexibility. This reflects a pragmatic approach by both parties and reserves institutional space for deepening cooperation based on actual operational experience.

The design of "immediate effect + periodic review + negotiated extension" means that the scope of cooperation may progressively expand without the need for new instruments. Manufacturers should be aware that what appears today as a framework arrangement may, within one to two years, develop into more operational technical annexes or joint guidance documents.

## 3. Impact Analysis

### (1) Impact on Medical Device Manufacturers

For manufacturers operating simultaneously in the EU and Singapore/ASEAN markets, the following points warrant particular attention:

First, consistency management of post-market surveillance data will become increasingly important. EU MDR Article 10(9) and Article 83 require manufacturers to establish systematic post-market surveillance systems, while HSA similarly requires marketing authorization holders to fulfill adverse event reporting and FSCA reporting obligations. With the activation of information exchange mechanisms between the two parties, the speed of "cross-comparison" of safety signals for the same product between the two regulatory databases will increase. If PMS data exhibit inconsistencies or deliberate selective reporting, the risk of cross-verification by multinational regulators will rise significantly. Manufacturers should ensure that global PMS data architectures support rapid response and consistent presentation across multiple regulatory jurisdictions.

Second, clinical evaluation and clinical trial strategies require reassessment. EU MDR Article 61 imposes substantially higher requirements for clinical evaluation than the former Medical Devices Directive (MDD 93/42/EEC), and many Class III and implantable devices face compliance challenges due to insufficient clinical data. In this context, the strategic value of conducting clinical investigations in Singapore and the broader ASEAN region to collect real-world clinical data is rising. The clause on clinical trial authorization information exchange in the Working Arrangement suggests that greater coordination in ethics review, trial design, and data recognition may emerge in the future. Manufacturers should evaluate the feasibility and strategic value of supplementing clinical data in the ASEAN region.

Third, dual-compliance costs for labelling and document management are likely to decrease in the medium to long term, but cautious handling is needed in the short term. While this Arrangement does not establish a labelling recognition mechanism, information exchange on labelling policies between the parties lays the groundwork for future convergence in labelling formats. During the transitional period, manufacturers must continue to separately satisfy Annex I of EU MDR and Singapore's Health Products (Medical Devices) Regulations 2010 labelling requirements. It would be imprudent to assume that a single set of labels can simultaneously cover both markets.

### (2) Impact on the Industry Landscape

At the industry level, this Working Arrangement sends a clear signal of regulatory convergence. Both the EU and Singapore are IMDRF members, and their bilateral cooperation will further strengthen coordination efforts under the IMDRF framework, particularly in the areas of the Medical Device Single Audit Program (MDSAP) and IMDRF technical documentation. For global manufacturers that rely on HSA registration in Singapore as a "reference" for the ASEAN market, the deepening of HSA's relationship with EU regulatory agencies may lead HSA's future review practices to further align with EU MDR standards, indirectly raising the market access threshold for the ASEAN region.

For small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), this trend is a double-edged sword. On one hand, regulatory convergence may reduce redundant multi-market compliance work; on the other hand, if HSA review standards move closer to EU MDR's high requirements, the relatively flexible Singapore registration pathway may become more stringent. SMEs should closely monitor HSA for any subsequent signals of changes in review guidance.

### (3) Impact on Patients and End Users

For patients and end users, the positive significance of this Working Arrangement is primarily reflected in safety signal response speed and product quality assurance. Cross-jurisdictional safety information flows can accelerate the identification of device safety issues on a global scale. In particular, when one market first detects an adverse event trend, the regulatory response in the other market can be significantly expedited. This "safety information first" mechanism design inherently serves public health interests, helping to reduce the risk exposure window caused by information asymmetry.

Furthermore, the enhancement of regulatory science capacity fostered by the training and knowledge-sharing programmes is expected to increase the technical depth and consistency of regulatory review in both jurisdictions, indirectly improving the quality standards and safety margins of approved products.

## 4. Compliance Recommendations

Based on the foregoing analysis, the following specific actions are recommended for affected medical device enterprises:

### (1) Establish a Dedicated Monitoring Mechanism for EU-Singapore Cooperation Developments

Regulatory affairs teams should incorporate EU-HSA cooperation developments into their routine monitoring scope, with particular attention to EMA and HSA official websites, DG SANTE communications, and any subsequent joint statements or technical guidance issued by both parties. It is recommended that a designated individual be assigned to review cooperation progress quarterly and assess its potential impact on the company's product portfolio in both markets. Particular attention should be paid to the technical domains addressed by "cluster activities"—if such activities involve categories relevant to the company's product lines, an internal impact assessment should be initiated immediately.

### (2) Review and Optimize Data Consistency in Post-Market Surveillance Systems

Review the global data architecture of the company's PMS system to ensure that adverse event, FSCA, trend reporting, and safety signal data are presented consistently and can be shared rapidly between the EU and Singapore/ASEAN markets. It is recommended that a systematic gap analysis be conducted against the requirements of EU MDR Articles 83–86 and Articles 87–92, as well as HSA's specific guidance on adverse event reporting and FSCA reporting. Cross-border data inconsistencies should be treated as the highest-priority remediation item.

### (3) Reassess Clinical Evaluation and ASEAN Clinical Data Strategies

In conjunction with the clinical evaluation requirements of EU MDR Article 61, seriously evaluate the strategic feasibility of supplementing clinical research in Singapore and the ASEAN region. For Class IIb and Class III devices with insufficient clinical data, clinical studies in the ASEAN region can serve as a valuable supplementary source for meeting EU MDR clinical evidence requirements. It is recommended to assess HSA clinical trial approval procedures early (with reference to Singapore's Health Products (Clinical Trials) Regulations) and incorporate them into the global clinical evidence generation plan.

### (4) Parallel-Track Strategy for Labelling and Document Management

In the short term, continue to maintain independent compliance management of the EU and Singapore labelling systems. It would be inappropriate to unilaterally unify labels without a clear mutual recognition arrangement in place. However, at the internal document management level, a modular architecture for labelling master data may be established to preserve flexibility for potential future labelling convergence. Monitor subsequent information releases from both parties regarding labelling policies.

### (5) Strengthen Supply Chain and Contract Management

Ensure that distributors, authorized representatives, importers, and contract manufacturers understand the potential changes in information flows arising from the EU-HSA cooperation. In EU authorized representative agreements (EU MDR Article 11) and Singapore local registrant agreements, it is recommended to review information-sharing clauses to ensure that each responsible entity can respond promptly and accurately when regulatory authorities request cross-border information provision.

### (6) Leverage Training Resources to Enhance Team Regulatory Science Capability

Closely monitor joint training or knowledge-sharing activities that EMA and HSA may organize. Such activities typically open a limited number of seats to industry participants and represent valuable opportunities to gain an in-depth understanding of both regulatory approaches. It is recommended that core regulatory affairs or clinical affairs personnel be assigned to participate in relevant activities, with learnings subsequently converted into internal training materials.

## 5. Timeline and Key Dates

The Working Arrangement was signed on **11 November 2026** and took effect **immediately**, with no transitional or grace period. The document expressly provides for **periodic review**, although no specific review interval is stipulated.

For medical device manufacturers, the following key milestones are recommended for attention:

- **11 November 2026**: Working Arrangement signed and effective immediately. Enterprises should treat this date as the initiation point for internal compliance assessment.
- **Q4 2026 through Q1 2027**: It is recommended that enterprises complete a first-round impact assessment within six months of signing, with particular focus on PMS data consistency and clinical evidence strategy.
- **Ongoing Monitoring**: Given the periodic review mechanism, enterprises are advised to continuously monitor EMA and HSA cooperation announcements on a six-month cycle, particularly any signals of "cluster activities" or joint technical document initiation.
- **Cross-Reference with EU MDR Timelines**: Enterprises should integrate the potential impact of this Arrangement with the remaining obligations under EU MDR transitional periods, ensuring that compliance roadmaps possess sufficient flexibility to absorb future regulatory harmonization changes.

In summary, while the EU-Singapore Working Arrangement is a framework-level administrative document, the mechanisms it establishes across the three dimensions of information exchange, expert participation, and capacity-building have laid a solid institutional track for medical device regulatory collaboration. Astute regulatory affairs professionals should not view it as a purely symbolic instrument but rather as an emerging signal of regulatory convergence—and should use it as an opportunity to proactively adjust multi-market compliance strategies.
